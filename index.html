The Book of Life

Inside the center part of each cell, there is a very long string of something we call life letters.

This string is written using only four letters: A C G T

These four letters, put together in the right order, tell your body how to build everything about you — your eyes, your hair, your blood, all of it.

This string of letters is so long that if you could put it straight, it would reach from here to the sun and back — over six hundred times.

Your body is made of very, very small parts called cells. You have many million million of them.

Each cell is like a tiny room. And in the middle of each room, there is an even smaller round thing.

We call it the center part of the cell. Inside it is something very important.

But how do you read
something so small?

For a long time, this was very hard. The string of life letters is far too small to see, even with the best glass that makes things look big.

So people found a way. But first, they had to break it.

Cut It Into Pieces

The first thing a reading machine does is cut the long string of life letters into million small pieces.

Each piece is only a few hundred letters long.

Think of it like this: imagine you had a very long book, and you cut every page into many small bits of paper. Now you have a million little bits of sentence, and no idea what order they go in.

A Field of Small Lights

The company that does most of the reading is called Illumina. Their machine is something very beautiful.

Each of those million pieces of life letters is read one letter at a time. Each of the four letters — A C G T — has its own color of light.

When a letter is added, it lights up for just a moment. A small eye watches and writes down the color. Then the next letter. Then the next.

This happens across million pieces at the same time. The machine becomes a field of tiny lights, turning on and off, like a field of small fire animals at evening time.

Putting the Book Back Together

Now you have million small pieces, each a few hundred letters long. A big thinking machine — a computer — takes all the pieces and tries to put them back in order.

It does this by looking at where pieces share the same letters at their ends. Where one piece ends with "...A T G C" and another starts with "A T G C...", the computer joins them together.

It's like putting a cut-up book back together by matching the ends of each sentence.

The Problem

But here is the problem. Some parts of the life letters are the same word, again and again and again, million times.

Imagine a page from a book that is nothing but the word "and" written over and over. If you cut that page into small pieces, every piece would look the same. You would have no way to know which piece came first.

The short pieces that the machine reads are not long enough to reach across these same-word parts.

What if the pieces
were much longer?

Two new ways of reading life letters changed everything. They can read pieces that are ten thousand or even million letters long.

Long enough to cross any same-word part.

The Small Dark Room

The first new way uses a very small dark room — so small you could fit a million of them side by side on your finger.

The life letters are joined into a circle, then put at the bottom of this tiny room. A strong light comes in from under, but the room is so small that the light only reaches the very bottom.

It's like a tiny dark room with a single bright light, and the life letters are the star of the show, right in the center of the light.

Because the letters are in a circle, the machine can read them again and again. If it makes a wrong read the first time, it can correct it on the next pass. This makes the reading very, very good.

Through the Tiny Hole

The second new way is very different. It does not use light at all. It uses power — the same kind that moves through a wire.

A very small hole sits in a thin wall. A tiny living machine pulls a single string of life letters through the hole, one letter at a time.

As each letter passes through, it changes the flow of power through the hole in a special way. Each of the four letters changes it differently. A thinking machine watches the changes and writes down each letter.

This way is so simple that the reading machine can be as small as your finger! And it can read pieces that are million letters long.

2000

People first tried to read all the life letters of one person. It took ten years and cost three thousand million dollars. And it was still not complete — eight parts out of a hundred were still missing.

2022

With the new long-read machines, people finally read every single letter — nothing missing. And it cost only two hundred dollars.

With better and less hard to use tools, people will be able to read the life letters of more and more of us. And learn more of the things that make each of us who we are.
