The Long Read Revolution
In 2000, the White House announced the completion of the decades-long, $3 billion Human Genome Project to sequence one human genome. Yet, the published results were missing 8% of our DNA. Scientists knew those regions existed, but they simply could not sequence them.

The reason for this lay in how DNA had been sequenced. Up until now, genome sequencing–the technology used to read an organism’s DNA, also known as its genome–has relied on a kind of controlled destruction. Illumina, the company that has dominated the field so far, makes machines that cut DNA into millions of fragments a few hundred letters long. Each fragment is copied one base at a time, and each of DNA’s four letters, A, C, G, and T, carries a different fluorescent color. When a new base is added, the added base flashes. A camera records the color, and the process repeats. This occurs across millions of fragments simultaneously, making the machine a field of microscopic lights, flickering on and off, like a meadow of fireflies at twilight.
Since a collection of tiny sequences alone is not useful, researchers task computers with assembling the fragments by matching their overlapping ends, much like piecing together a shredded book from scraps of sentences.
But the human genome is notorious for containing passages that cannot easily be reconstructed from its scraps. Large regions, especially the sections at the ends and centers of chromosomes, contain short sequences repeated millions of times. A hundred-letter fragment from one of these regions carries about as much location information as the word “and” torn from a page filled entirely with “and.”
Thus, the fragments that the machines read need to be long enough to cover an entire repetitive region. Analogously, to bridge a river, a plank needs to be long enough to reach both banks. For years, sequencing technologies could rarely produce reads long enough to do this.
That all changed with long read sequencing. Companies Pacific Biosciences and Oxford Nanopore now manufacture machines that can read fragments that are tens of thousands or millions of bases long.
Pacific Biosciences, informally PacBio, reads DNA using fluorescence, like Illumina. A DNA fragment is joined into a circle and placed in a nanoscopic chamber, called a zero-mode waveguide. Laser light enters from below, but the well is so narrow that the light fades almost immediately, illuminating only the very tip of the bottom. The well acts like a tiny theater, with a spotlight fixed on the protagonist, the DNA itself. The circular DNA is then replicated at the bottom of the waveguide, using several proteins. As each letter is added, the laser illuminates it, and the color is recorded, just like for Illumina. This circular DNA gives PacBio a major advantage: the molecule can be copied multiple times. If a pass contains a mistake, later ones can correct it. This lets PacBio create incredibly accurate reads of long sequences.
Oxford Nanopore uses a radically different approach from PacBio’s or Illumina’s. It uses electricity. A tiny protein pore embedded in a membrane has a biological motor that pulls a strand of DNA through it. The membrane has voltage across it, and each base passing through the pore affects that voltage in a characteristic way. By measuring those changes and using an AI algorithm, Oxford Nanopore is able to read fragments of DNA millions of bases long. The simplicity of this method allows the sequencer to be as small as a thumb drive!
Challenges, of course, remain. Nanopore can struggle with long runs of the same letter because repeated letters produce nearly indistinguishable signals, making it difficult to determine exactly how many have passed through the pore.
Despite the difficulties, long reads finally gave researchers a way across regions that had resisted sequencing for decades. In 2022, 21 years after the Human Genome Project ended, scientists published the first gapless human genome, at a sequencing cost of just $200. With cheaper and more effective tools, scientists will be able to read the individual DNA of more and more people, and uncover more of the secrets we hold inside of us.
